Florida already has a red light. What it doesn't have is a yellow one.

I'm a forensic psychologist. I do court-ordered evaluations in Florida, and I care deeply about violence, gun laws, and how our systems respond to risk and trauma. The warning signs usually show up before the tragedy. The question is whether anyone with the power to act is allowed to.

That's why I'm advocating for the Violence Risk Reduction & Prevention Act (VRRPA).

## What the VRRPA is

The VRRPA, which I call Florida's Yellow Light Act, is a proposed Florida bill. It is a citizen-led proposal and has not been filed in the Legislature yet. It builds on the tool Florida already uses, the risk protection order (RPO), known in other states as an extreme risk protection order (ERPO) or a "red flag" law. These orders let a court temporarily remove firearms from someone who poses a serious danger to themselves or others.

As drafted, the VRRPA would:

- Let qualified mental health professionals petition for a risk protection order.
- Add a domestic violence addendum.
- Require training programs and a public education campaign.
- Require a hearing within 7 days of filing the petition.

## Florida already has the red light

Florida passed its risk protection order law in 2018, after the shooting at Marjory Stoneman Douglas High School. Gov. Rick Scott signed it on March 9, 2018, as part of the Marjory Stoneman Douglas High School Public Safety Act (NPR, 2019).

Under that law, only a law enforcement officer or agency can petition. A judge has to find clear and convincing evidence that the person poses a significant danger, after notice and a hearing held within 14 days. A final order lasts up to 12 months (Fla. Stat. § 790.401).

Courts have authorized more than 19,000 of these orders since the law took effect, according to the advocacy group Everytown (Florida Phoenix, 2026). The law is associated with an 11% drop in Florida's firearm homicide rate from 2019 to 2021 compared with similar states. The same study found no significant change in firearm suicide, and the authors were careful to say they couldn't rule out other post-Parkland changes (Gimbrone & Rudolph, 2024).

And in 2026, a House bill was filed to repeal risk protection orders entirely (Florida Phoenix, 2026). So this isn't only about building something new. It's about defending what works and finishing the job.

## The reasons

### Suicide is most of the problem

**1. Most gun deaths are suicides.** In 2024, 62% of U.S. gun deaths were suicides: 27,593 people, a new peak (Gramlich, 2026). When we talk about gun violence, we are mostly talking about suicide.

**2. Guns are the most lethal method.** About 9 out of 10 suicide attempts with a firearm end in death (Conner et al., 2019). Most people who survive a suicide attempt by other means get a second chance. With a gun, most don't.

**3. Access itself is the risk.** In a study of more than 26 million Californians, men who owned handguns had about 8 times the risk of firearm suicide of men who didn't. For women, it was about 35 times (Studdert et al., 2020). Removing access during a crisis is the point of an RPO.

**4. Removing guns in a crisis saves lives.** In Connecticut, researchers estimated one suicide was prevented for every 10 to 20 gun removals (Swanson et al., 2017). In Indiana, about one for every 10 (Swanson et al., 2019). A more recent and more conservative estimate puts it at one life saved for every 22 orders (Miller et al., 2024). Twenty-two court orders for one human life is a trade I'll take every time.

**5. State-level results point the same way, with honest caveats.** Indiana's law was associated with a 7.5% drop in firearm suicides in its first decade. Connecticut's was associated with a 13.7% drop once enforcement picked up, but part of that was offset by more suicides by other methods (Kivisto & Phalen, 2018). Not perfect. Still lives.

### These orders are already stopping threatened mass shootings

**6. They're being used for exactly this.** Across six states, including Florida, 10% of 6,787 ERPO cases were filed in response to a threat to kill three or more people. The most common target was a K-12 school. Judges granted 93% of the temporary orders in those cases (Zeoli et al., 2022).

**7. In California, the threatened shootings didn't happen.** In a series of 21 cases where an order was used to try to stop a mass shooting, none of the threatened shootings had occurred, and no homicides or suicides by the people under the orders were found. The researchers make no claim that the orders caused that, and nobody can know what would have happened without them (Wintemute et al., 2019).

### Risk, not diagnosis

**8. Mental illness isn't the predictor people think it is.** The large majority of people with serious mental illnesses are never violent toward others (Swanson et al., 2015). A diagnosis doesn't tell you who is dangerous this week. Behavior does. RPOs act on behavior, they're temporary, and a judge decides.

**9. Clinicians often see the warning signs first, and in Florida they can't petition.** Eight states, plus D.C. and the U.S. Virgin Islands, already let clinicians file for an extreme risk order (Houshmand et al., 2026). The VRRPA would give qualified mental health professionals that standing in Florida, so the person who hears the threat in the room isn't stuck waiting on someone else to act.

### Domestic violence and guns

**10. A gun in an abusive home is a death threat.** When an abuser has access to a gun, the risk that the abused woman is killed is about five times higher (Campbell et al., 2003).

**11. Domestic violence is a warning sign for mass violence.** In 68% of U.S. mass shootings from 2014 to 2019 (four or more people killed), the shooter either had a history of domestic violence or killed a partner or family member during the shooting (Geller et al., 2021).

**12. Partners are already being named in these threats.** In the six-state study, intimate partners and their children and families were the third most common target of threatened mass shootings, after schools and businesses (Zeoli et al., 2022). The VRRPA's domestic violence addendum is built for that reality.

### Doing it right

**13. A law nobody knows about doesn't protect anybody.** In a survey of Maryland physicians, about 72% said they were not at all familiar with their state's ERPO law, and only one had ever filed a petition (Frattaroli et al., 2019). Clinicians name lack of awareness and training as a top barrier (Houshmand et al., 2026). The VRRPA requires training and public education for that reason.

**14. Due process is built in.** The VRRPA requires a hearing within 7 days, half of Florida's current 14. Fast protection and a fast day in court go together. The U.S. Supreme Court has said that "an individual found by a court to pose a credible threat to the physical safety of another may be temporarily disarmed consistent with the Second Amendment" (*United States v. Rahimi*, 2024). That case was about domestic violence restraining orders, not ERPOs, but the principle is the same one these laws rest on: a judge, a finding, and a time limit.

**15. It's a public health priority.** The Johns Hopkins Center for Gun Violence Solutions names firearm removal laws like ERPOs as one of five priority solutions in its goal to cut gun violence 30% by 2030 (Johns Hopkins Center for Gun Violence Solutions, 2024).

## What the evidence can't say yet

I'm not going to oversell this. RAND's ongoing review rates the evidence that ERPOs reduce suicide as limited, and the evidence on mass shootings and violent crime as inconclusive (RAND, 2026). Most of these studies are young, and the laws are newer still.

Limited isn't zero. The suicide findings point in one direction across states and research teams. The cost of waiting for perfect evidence is counted in funerals.
