Every person is impacted by the wage gap. Most people are unaware that it is happening.

And when it does come up, someone always says it: the gap is exaggerated, or women could close it if they just worked harder.

That's a myth. The wage gap is real, it's systemic, and it's far more complicated than individual effort. Here's the evidence.

## The gap in numbers

In 2025, women working full-time, year-round in the U.S. earned about 84 cents for every dollar men earned (Kollar & Scherer, 2026). That's up from about 81 cents in 2024, and it's still a gap.

"Women just choose lower-paying jobs." Some of the gap is about which jobs and industries men and women end up in. Not all of it. When economists account for education, experience, occupation, industry, and more, part of the gap is still left over. In one major analysis of U.S. data through 2010, about 38% of the gap couldn't be explained by any of those factors (Blau & Kahn, 2017).

## Same job, less pay

Look at medicine, where men and women have the same degrees. At 24 U.S. public medical schools, women physicians earned about $20,000 less per year than men, after accounting for age, experience, specialty, faculty rank, research output, and clinical revenue (Jena et al., 2016).

Same training. Same work. Smaller paycheck.

## The motherhood penalty and the fatherhood bonus

Having a child costs women money. On average, women's earnings drop about 4% for each child, even after accounting for experience, education, and job characteristics. For low-wage women, the penalty is bigger (Budig, 2014; Budig & Hodges, 2010).

Men get the opposite. Fatherhood raises men's earnings by more than 6% on average (Budig, 2014).

Bias is part of why. In one experiment, people rated equally qualified job applicants who differed only in whether they had children. Mothers were offered about $11,000 less in starting salary than women without children. Fathers were offered about $6,000 more than men without children. Then the researchers sent matched résumés to real employers: childless women got about twice as many callbacks as equally qualified mothers. Fathers weren't penalized at all (Correll et al., 2007).

Mothers get seen as less committed. Fathers get seen as more stable. Neither has anything to do with actual performance.

## Bias starts before the first paycheck

In a well-known study, 127 science professors reviewed an application for a lab manager job. The application was identical, except half were named John and half were named Jennifer. Professors rated John as more competent and offered him about $30,200, compared with about $26,500 for Jennifer. Women professors did it too (Moss-Racusin et al., 2012).

No amount of "working harder" fixes a lower number for the same résumé.

## Negotiating doesn't save you either

"Then women should just ask for more." When women do, they're often penalized for it. In experiments, evaluators were less willing to work with a woman who negotiated her salary, and saw her as demanding. Men who negotiated didn't face that cost (Bowles et al., 2007).

So women are penalized for not negotiating, and penalized for negotiating. That's not a skills gap. It's a double bind.

## Harassment costs money, too

In a 2017 Pew survey, 42% of working women said they'd experienced gender discrimination at work. One in four said they'd earned less than a man doing the same job. And 22% said they'd been sexually harassed at work, compared with 7% of men (Parker & Funk, 2017).

Harassment doesn't just make work miserable. It changes careers. In one long-term study, women who were harassed were 6.5 times more likely to change jobs, and they reported more financial stress two years later (McLaughlin et al., 2017).

## The glass ceiling, and the glass cliff

Women run 56 of the Fortune 500 companies in 2026. That's a record, and it's 11.2% (Hinchliffe, 2026).

When women do get to the top, it's often at the worst possible moment. Researchers call this the glass cliff: women are more likely than men to be picked to lead organizations that are already struggling, which makes success harder and failure more visible (Ryan & Haslam, 2005; Haslam & Ryan, 2008).

## The second shift

Here's the part "work harder" really misses. Women already do.

On an average day in 2025, 87% of U.S. women did household work, compared with 75% of men. In homes with children under 6, women spent about an hour more per day than men on direct childcare (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2026). Across OECD countries, women do about 2 more hours of unpaid work per day than men, and when you add paid and unpaid work together, women work more in total (OECD, 2020).

That extra shift has to come from somewhere. Often it comes out of paid hours, promotions, and the jobs women feel they can take on.

## The solution isn't "working harder"

Structural barriers like bias, discrimination, harassment, and unequal home expectations can't be overcome by individual effort alone.

Closing the gap takes changes to the system: pay transparency, real family leave for everyone, an end to sexism and harassment at work, and a cultural shift in who does the work at home.

## Try this

Talk about pay. In the U.S., most private-sector employees have a legal right to discuss wages with coworkers under the National Labor Relations Act (NLRB, n.d.). Ask one trusted coworker what they make, and tell them what you make. Secrecy protects the gap.

## Recommended reads

- *Invisible Women: Exposing Data Bias in a World Designed for Men* by Caroline Criado Perez (2019). How decisions built on male-default data shape women's lives, including at work.
- *Career and Family: Women's Century-Long Journey toward Equity* by Claudia Goldin (2021). A Nobel-winning economist on why the gap is built into how jobs are structured, not how hard women try.
- *The Second Shift* by Arlie Hochschild (1989). The classic on the unpaid work that starts when the paid workday ends.

The data is clear. Women are working hard. It's the system that needs to change.
