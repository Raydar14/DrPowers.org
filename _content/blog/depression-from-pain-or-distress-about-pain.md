Chronic pain and depression show up together all the time. In a VA claim, a forensic report, or a treatment plan, the question isn't whether they're connected. It's how.

Two DSM-5-TR diagnoses are often confused here: depressive disorder due to another medical condition, and somatic symptom disorder with predominant pain. They describe different relationships between pain and mental health. Getting the name right matters for treatment, and it matters for how the record reads.

## First, a correction on the name

You'll sometimes see "depressive disorder due to chronic pain." That isn't a DSM-5-TR diagnosis.

The diagnosis is **depressive disorder due to another medical condition**, and the clinician names the condition in the diagnosis itself (for example, "depressive disorder due to [the specific medical condition]"). Pain is a symptom. The diagnosis points to the medical condition that's causing it.

## Depressive disorder due to another medical condition

This diagnosis is about **physiology**. In DSM-5-TR terms, there has to be evidence from the history, physical examination, or laboratory findings that the mood disturbance is "the direct pathophysiological consequence of another medical condition" (American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2022).

In plain English: the medical condition is changing the brain and body in a way that produces depression. Classic examples are hypothyroidism and Parkinson's disease.

The criteria also require that:

- The depressed mood or loss of interest is prominent and persistent.
- It isn't better explained by another mental disorder.
- It doesn't happen only during a delirium.
- It causes clinically significant distress or impairment.

The specifiers are "with depressive features," "with major depressive-like episode," and "with mixed features" (APA, 2022).

### What this diagnosis is not

Here's where a lot of records go wrong. Being depressed *because* you live with pain (because you lost your job, your sleep, your sense of who you are) is a psychological reaction to a medical condition. That is real, and it matters. But it isn't what "due to another medical condition" means.

Living with a serious medical condition is itself a major life stressor. When depression is a reaction to that stressor, the better fit may be an adjustment disorder or a major depressive disorder, not a "due to" diagnosis (APA, 2022). Clinicians weigh things like timing (was there depression before the medical condition?), whether the mood improves when the condition is treated, and whether the condition is known to cause depression physiologically.

## Somatic symptom disorder with predominant pain

This diagnosis is about **how a person relates to their symptoms**. It requires one or more distressing somatic symptoms, plus excessive thoughts, feelings, or behaviors about them, shown by at least one of these (APA, 2022):

1. Disproportionate and persistent thoughts about how serious the symptoms are.
2. Persistently high anxiety about health or symptoms.
3. Excessive time and energy spent on the symptoms or health concerns.

The symptomatic state is typically persistent for more than six months. "With predominant pain" is a specifier for people whose somatic symptoms are mainly pain. It replaced the older DSM-IV diagnosis of pain disorder (APA, 2013).

Severity is rated mild, moderate, or severe, based on how many of the three features above are present, and there's a "persistent" specifier for severe symptoms with marked impairment lasting more than six months.

### Two common misunderstandings

**It does not mean the pain is "in your head."** Somatic symptom disorder doesn't require the pain to be medically unexplained. Symptoms "may or may not be associated with another medical condition" (APA, 2013). A veteran with a documented back injury can meet the criteria.

**It is not diagnosed just because no medical cause was found.** The APA is explicit that it isn't appropriate to diagnose a mental disorder solely because a medical cause can't be demonstrated (APA, 2013). The diagnosis rests on the excessive thoughts, feelings, or behaviors, not on the absence of a medical explanation.

## The difference, side by side

| | Depressive disorder due to another medical condition | Somatic symptom disorder with predominant pain |
|---|---|---|
| Core problem | A depressive syndrome | Distress and excessive focus on pain |
| Role of the medical condition | Directly causes the depression through physiology | May or may not be present; not required |
| What must be shown | Evidence the mood change is a direct pathophysiological result of the condition | Excessive thoughts, feelings, or behaviors about the pain, typically for more than 6 months |
| Not this diagnosis if | Depression is a psychological reaction to illness (consider adjustment disorder or major depressive disorder) | The only basis is "we couldn't find a medical cause" |
| Treatment emphasis | Treat the underlying condition and the depression | Address the thoughts, fears, and behaviors that amplify pain and disability |

The two can also coexist with other diagnoses. That's a clinical judgment, made on the record and the examination.

## Why this matters in a VA claim

VA rates mental disorders under 38 CFR § 4.130 using one scale, the General Rating Formula for Mental Disorders. Ratings run 0, 10, 30, 50, 70, and 100 percent, based on the level of "occupational and social impairment" (38 C.F.R. § 4.130). VA's rating schedule uses DSM-5 nomenclature. If a mental disorder diagnosis doesn't conform to DSM-5 or isn't supported by the examination, VA is required to return the report to the examiner (38 C.F.R. § 4.125).

A few points worth knowing:

**The diagnosis name doesn't set the percentage. The impairment does.** VA considers the frequency, severity, and duration of symptoms, the length of remissions, and capacity for adjustment, based on all the evidence of record, not only the examination (38 C.F.R. § 4.126(a)).

**Somatic symptom disorder has its own diagnostic code.** It's listed as code 9421. Depressive disorders are listed as major depressive disorder (9434), persistent depressive disorder (9433), and unspecified depressive disorder (9435). There's no separate code titled "due to another medical condition" for depression (38 C.F.R. § 4.130).

**Overlap is handled on purpose.** When a single disability is diagnosed as both a physical condition and a mental disorder, VA rates it under the code for the dominant, more disabling aspect (38 C.F.R. § 4.126(d)). VA also avoids rating the same disability, or the same symptoms, twice under different diagnoses (38 C.F.R. § 4.14). Pain and psychological distress can overlap, and the record should be clear about which symptoms belong to which condition.

**Secondary service connection.** A disability that is "proximately due to or the result of" a service-connected disease or injury is itself service connected. So is any increase in severity of a non-service-connected condition caused by a service-connected one, beyond its natural progress (38 C.F.R. § 3.310). For depression connected to a service-connected pain condition, the question is the link, and the link has to be supported in the evidence.

A proposed update to VA's mental disorders rating schedule was published in 2022. As of this writing, the General Rating Formula above is what's in effect. Check the current eCFR before relying on it.

## What a careful evaluation looks for

- The timeline: mood, pain, and the medical condition, in order.
- Whether there's a known physiological pathway from the condition to depression.
- How the person thinks about, worries about, and organizes their life around the pain.
- Whether the mood tracks the medical condition's treatment, or runs on its own course.
- Functional impact at work and at home, described concretely.

## Try this

If you're a veteran or a representative reviewing a record, find the exact diagnosis wording in every mental health note and exam. Write each one down, word for word. If they don't match each other, or don't match the DSM-5-TR name, that's worth raising with the examiner or your representative.

The name sets the question. The evidence answers it. Precision protects both.
