One tap and they're gone. No more texts. No more seeing their stories. No more feeling whatever you were feeling when their name lit up your phone.

Blocking is fast, and sometimes it's exactly right. Sometimes it's a way to skip a conversation you need to have. The tap looks the same. What's behind it isn't.

## Boundary or avoidance?

A boundary protects you from something. Avoidance protects you from a feeling.

Blocking is a boundary when the contact itself is the problem: they won't stop, they won't respect a no, or every exchange leaves you worse. You've said what you needed to say, or you don't owe them anything more.

Blocking is avoidance when the contact isn't the problem, the conversation is. You're angry, embarrassed, or hurt, and blocking means you never have to sit with it. It feels like control. Often it's a way of not feeling.

Ask yourself one question before you tap: *Am I protecting my safety and my peace, or am I protecting myself from a hard conversation?*

## What it does to the person blocked

For the person on the other side, a block can land hard. One day there's a connection, the next there's silence, with no explanation and no chance to respond.

That often brings up rejection, confusion, and a pile of unanswered questions. The relationship ends without an ending.

That doesn't mean you owe anyone access to you. It means that when it's safe, the person you're blocking is still a person, and how you leave matters.

## Try a time-out first

If you're in the middle of a fight, you probably don't need a block. You need a break.

- **Say it out loud.** "I need to step away from this. I'm not ending things. I'll reach out on Sunday."
- **Give it a return date.** A pause with an end point lets both of you calm down without either of you wondering if it's over.
- **Mute instead of block.** Muting stops the notifications without slamming the door.
- **Come back when you said you would.** That's what makes a time-out trustworthy.

If you keep needing the block button with the same person, that's information. It may be time for a real conversation about the relationship, or for help from a couples therapist.

## When blocking is the right call

Sometimes there's nothing to talk through. Block, and don't look back, when someone:

- Threatens you, or makes you afraid for your safety.
- Harasses you, or keeps contacting you after you've asked them to stop.
- Is abusive, emotionally, physically, sexually, or financially.
- Uses contact to control, monitor, or manipulate you.

In those situations, blocking isn't avoidance. It's protection. You don't owe an explanation, a goodbye, or a second chance.

Save the messages before you block, in case you need them later. If you're in danger in the U.S., call 911. For support and safety planning, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788. Laws on harassment and protective orders vary by state and country, so a local advocate or attorney can tell you what applies where you live.

## Try this

Next time your thumb is hovering over block, wait ten minutes. Ask the question: safety and peace, or a hard conversation? Then decide.

It's about understanding the impact on others and not overly protecting yourself from feelings.

Pause. Decide. Then tap, or talk.
