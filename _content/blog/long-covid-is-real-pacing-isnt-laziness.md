Long COVID is real. It's not in your head, it's not a character flaw, and resting is not the same as giving up.

Dr. Bruce Patterson understands Long COVID like few others. His research and treatment have been personally life-changing for me, and it's my goal to help people recognize the symptoms and harms so we can collectively get better.

That's why I'm writing this. Here's what we know, what we don't, and what you can do right now.

## What Long COVID is

In 2024, the U.S. National Academies defined Long COVID as a chronic condition that starts after a COVID-19 infection and lasts at least three months. It can be continuous, it can come and go, or it can slowly get worse. It can affect one organ system or several (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine [NASEM], 2024).

The CDC says symptoms can come, go, and come back over weeks, months, and even years, and that Long COVID can sometimes cause disability. Anyone who gets COVID-19 can develop it, including children (CDC, 2026a).

## It's not rare

In a 2022 U.S. survey, 6.9% of adults said they had ever had Long COVID, and 3.4% had it at the time they were asked (Adjaye-Gbewonyo et al., 2023). Researchers estimate at least 65 million people worldwide have had it, and likely more, since many infections were never counted (Davis et al., 2023).

## What it can feel like

There's no single Long COVID experience. More than 200 symptoms have been identified (CDC, 2026b). Some of the most common:

- Fatigue that gets in the way of daily life
- Symptoms that get worse after physical or mental effort (post-exertional malaise, or PEM)
- Brain fog: trouble thinking, focusing, or remembering
- Shortness of breath, chest pain, or a racing heart
- Dizziness or lightheadedness when you stand up
- Sleep problems and headaches
- Joint or muscle pain
- Changes in smell or taste
- Stomach problems
- Depression or anxiety

Symptoms range from mild to completely disabling.

## What it does to a life

Long COVID can make it hard to work or go to school. It can make basic tasks like cooking, showering, or walking to the mailbox feel like a mountain. It strains relationships, because the illness is often invisible and misunderstood. And lost income plus medical bills create real financial stress.

Brain fog alone can derail a career. Losing the ability to multitask, or to handle a normal workload, is not a small thing.

## What might be going on in the body

The honest answer: scientists don't know yet, and there's probably not one single cause. Researchers are studying several overlapping possibilities (Davis et al., 2023):

- **Leftover virus.** Pieces of the virus may hang around in the body's tissues after the infection is over.
- **An immune system that won't settle.** Some people show signs of ongoing immune activation, and sometimes old dormant viruses (like Epstein-Barr) wake back up.
- **Autoimmunity.** The immune system may start targeting the body's own tissues.
- **Tiny clots and blood vessel damage.** Small clots and injury to the lining of blood vessels may reduce oxygen delivery.
- **Nervous system dysregulation.** Problems with the autonomic nervous system, which runs heart rate, blood pressure, and digestion, may explain the dizziness and racing heart when standing.
- **Gut microbiome changes.**

These ideas aren't competing. Different people may have different mixes.

## Dr. Patterson's work

Dr. Bruce Patterson is a physician-scientist and former Stanford virologist. His company, IncellDx (now HealthBio), focuses on measuring immune changes in people with Long COVID.

**What his research found.** In a 2022 study, his team found pieces of the virus's spike protein (the S1 subunit) inside a type of immune cell called non-classical monocytes in 19 of 26 people with Long COVID, in samples taken up to 15 months after infection (Patterson et al., 2022). The idea in plain English: if viral protein stays inside immune cells, it may keep poking the immune system and driving inflammation.

That was a small study, and several authors work for his company. It's an important finding that needs independent confirmation.

**His treatment approach.** His group uses detailed immune blood tests to guide treatment, sometimes with maraviroc (a drug that blocks a receptor called CCR5) combined with a statin. In a case series of 18 patients treated with maraviroc and pravastatin, symptoms improved on several scales over 6 to 12 weeks (Patterson et al., 2023).

Here's what that study can't tell us. There was no comparison group. Many patients had tried other treatments before. And the authors themselves say a placebo-controlled trial is still needed. Dr. Patterson's company also holds a patent on this use of maraviroc (Patterson et al., 2023).

That larger trial is now underway. A randomized, placebo-controlled trial of maraviroc plus atorvastatin, sponsored by his company, plans to enroll 252 people (HealthBio Therapeutics, 2025). Results aren't out yet.

So here's where I land. Dr. Patterson's work changed things for me. The science behind it is promising, biologically plausible, and still unproven in large independent trials. It isn't standard care yet. If you're interested, talk with a physician who knows the research and the risks, ideally in a trial or a closely monitored medical setting.

## What mainstream research is doing

The National Institutes of Health runs a large program called RECOVER to study Long COVID and test treatments. Its trials arm, RECOVER-TLC, is currently testing baricitinib (an anti-inflammatory drug), with trials of low-dose naltrexone, semaglutide, and nerve blocks in the works (RECOVER, 2026).

Some things that seemed promising haven't held up. In a randomized trial, a 15-day course of the antiviral Paxlovid did not improve Long COVID symptoms compared with placebo (Geng et al., 2024).

As of now, there are no broadly effective treatments for Long COVID (Davis et al., 2023). That's frustrating. It's also why trials matter.

## What you can do right now

This is information, not medical advice. **See a physician.** Long COVID is complicated, and you deserve a full evaluation from someone who takes it seriously.

### 1. Get properly evaluated

- Start with a primary care clinician who believes you. Bring a symptom list, a timeline, and how it affects your work and daily life.
- Ask whether there's a Long COVID or post-COVID clinic near you.
- Ask about ruling out other conditions that can look like Long COVID or make it worse, such as anemia, thyroid problems, or sleep apnea.

### 2. Respect the crash

If effort makes you feel worse a day or two later, that's post-exertional malaise. Pushing through can make it worse. For people with Long COVID who have PEM, exercise can be harmful and shouldn't be used as a treatment (Davis et al., 2023).

What helps many people is **pacing**: planning your activity so you stay inside your energy limits. The CDC describes it as avoiding "push-and-crash" cycles, like when you feel good one day, do too much, and pay for it for days (CDC, n.d.).

- Break tasks into smaller pieces.
- Rest before you're wiped out, not after.
- Sit down for tasks you'd normally stand for, like showering or folding laundry.
- Some people track heart rate or steps to keep effort steady.

### 3. Work on symptoms with your care team

- **Fatigue:** pacing, and rehab from therapists who understand PEM. Not boot-camp rehab.
- **Breathing problems:** ask about supervised pulmonary rehab.
- **Dizziness and racing heart:** ask your doctor about fluids, salt, compression garments, and whether medication makes sense for you. Some of these aren't safe for everyone, so don't start them on your own.
- **Brain fog:** cognitive rehab, shorter work blocks, written reminders, simpler task lists, and work accommodations like reduced hours or remote work.

### 4. Take care of your mind, too

Long COVID is a physical illness. It can also be an emotional trauma. Losing your energy, your work, or your sense of who you are is a real loss, and anxiety and depression often follow.

Look for a therapist who treats Long COVID as real. Not "it's all in your head." Peer support groups can help you feel less alone.

### 5. Be careful with experimental treatments

- Don't self-prescribe prescription drugs or stack medications you found online.
- Try new treatments only with medical supervision, ideally in a clinical trial.
- Look for trials at [RECOVER](https://recovercovid.org/) and [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/) (search "long COVID").

## Everyday care, without toxic positivity

None of this will cure Long COVID on its own. It can support your body while science catches up.

- **Rest as medicine.** Pacing and rest are medical tools, not laziness.
- **Gentle nervous system care.** Breathwork within your limits, grounding, body-based practices.
- **Food you can tolerate.** Regular meals, enough protein.
- **Sleep protection.** A steady routine and a dark room.
- **Boundaries.** With work, with family, with yourself. Accommodations are not weakness.

## Try this today

Write down your three most important tasks for tomorrow. Then cut one. Plan a rest break before each of the other two, not after.

## For skeptical friends and family

Long COVID is invisible, so people around you may not get it. Dr. Patterson explains his model in plain language in this [research update](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IvWPT3YA5uY). NIH's [RECOVER research summaries](https://recovercovid.org/research-summaries) are a good place to show people this is real.

You're not lazy. You're not making it up. You're sick, and you deserve care.

Rest. Pace. Keep going.
