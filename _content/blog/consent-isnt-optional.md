Consent isn't optional. It's the foundation. From kink to court, real power starts with "yes."

Not a mumbled, pressured "I guess." A real yes, from someone who knows what they're agreeing to, wants it, and can change their mind at any point.

That isn't a buzzkill. That's how good sex works.

## What consent is

Consent is a voluntary, enthusiastic, and mutual agreement to engage in sexual activity. It isn't one moment. It's an ongoing process of communication: before, during, and after.

Five things make it real. Planned Parenthood teaches the same five with the acronym FRIES (Planned Parenthood, n.d.).

**Voluntary.** Given freely, without coercion or pressure. A yes you got by wearing someone down, guilting them, or threatening the relationship isn't a yes.

**Informed.** Everyone understands what they're agreeing to, including the details that change the risk: condoms, contraception, other partners, STI status. If one person is hiding something that would change the other person's answer, it isn't informed. (When the hidden thing is a condom coming off, I have a name for it: condom rape.)

**Enthusiastic.** A positive, willing attitude. You want to, not you feel like you're supposed to.

**Specific.** Consent for one activity doesn't mean consent for all activities. Kissing isn't a yes to sex. Sex with a condom isn't a yes to sex without one.

**Reversible.** Anyone can change their mind at any point, including in the middle. When consent is withdrawn, you stop. Right away, no debate.

## Myths to drop

- **Silence isn't consent.** No answer is not an answer.
- **Previous consent doesn't guarantee future consent.** Yes last week isn't yes tonight.
- **Clothes, flirting, and location aren't consent.** Neither is your history together (Planned Parenthood, n.d.).

## How to ask

Talk before you're in the moment. Desires, boundaries, comfort levels, safer sex, contraception. It's easier to say what you want (and don't) with your clothes on.

Ask clearly, in plain words. "Is this okay?" "Do you want to keep going?" "Do you want me to...?"

Then make it easy to say no. If no would cost someone something (your mood, the night, the relationship), their yes doesn't mean much.

Check in during, especially when something changes: a new activity, more intensity, a different pace. A quick "Still good?" takes two seconds.

Asking doesn't kill the mood. Asking is foreplay.

## How to say no

You don't owe anyone a long explanation. "No." "Not tonight." "I want to stop." "I'm not into that, but I'd like..." Every one of those is a complete sentence.

Say your limits early, before things heat up, when they're easier to say and easier to hear. Practice being assertive about what you need. It sets the tone for both of you.

Respect your own boundaries, consistently. When a partner watches you honor your own comfort, they learn to honor it too.

And if you're the one who hears no: thank them. A partner who can say no is a partner whose yes you can trust.

## Body language counts. It isn't a contract.

Pay attention to nonverbal cues: pulling away, going still, going quiet, not kissing back, looking away. Respect them. Disregarding discomfort or hesitation is a mistake.

But body language isn't a substitute for words. It's easy to misread, especially in the dark, in a hurry, or after a few drinks. If there's any uncertainty, ask out loud.

## Capacity: who can say yes

Consent takes the capacity to give it. Three things can take that away.

### Alcohol and drugs

Alcohol impairs judgment and decision-making. It also blurs signals, so cues get misread and misunderstandings pile up. When alcohol is involved, rely on explicit words, not assumptions.

If someone is visibly intoxicated, they may not be in a position to make clear decisions. Err on the side of caution and wait. Planned Parenthood says it plainly: "People who are drunk, high, or passed out can't consent to sex" (Planned Parenthood, n.d.).

Where the law draws the line between "had a few" and "incapacitated" depends on your state or country. Don't bet someone's body, or your freedom, on guessing where it is.

Look out for one another. Notice signs of impairment in your friends, and step in if you need to.

### Age

Minors can't legally consent to sex with adults, and where that line sits depends on where you live. In the U.S., each state sets its own law. A federal review found that states with a single age of consent set it between 16 and 18, and many states look at the age difference between partners instead (Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation [ASPE], n.d.). Other countries set it differently.

### Power

When one person controls another person's job, grades, housing, treatment, or freedom, a yes can be shaped by fear of what no would cost. Boss and employee. Teacher and student. Therapist and client.

My own profession's ethics code is blunt about it: psychologists don't have sex with current clients, and not with former clients for at least two years, and even then almost never (American Psychological Association [APA], 2017). Some of these relationships are crimes no matter what anyone says, depending on where you are.

In any relationship, be mindful of power dynamics. Both partners should feel equal, and free to say what they want and don't want without fear of consequences.

## Consent in long-term relationships

Being together isn't a standing yes. Dating, married, ten years in: couples need to consent every time (Planned Parenthood, n.d.).

Marital rape has been illegal in all 50 states since 1993, though some states still treat rape within a marriage differently from other rape (Ebbert, 2019). The law took a long time to say what should have been obvious. Your marriage isn't consent. Your partner's body still belongs to your partner.

Long-term partners stop asking because they think they already know. Sometimes they do. Check anyway. Keep talking about desires and boundaries as you both change.

## Consent in kink and at sexy parties

Kink isn't the opposite of consent. Done well, it's consent at its most explicit.

The same rules apply at a play party, a sex club, or any adult gathering. A few matter even more:

- **Talk before you go.** Desires, boundaries, expectations, and anything that's off-limits.
- **Consent is ongoing.** It can be withdrawn at any point, and everyone should feel free to say so throughout the night.
- **Honor limits immediately.** If someone sets a limit or withdraws consent, respect it right away, without question or pressure.
- **Use safe words and signals.** Agree on them ahead of time, including nonverbal signals for loud or crowded spaces.
- **In groups, check in with everyone.** More people means more chances someone's no gets lost. Make sure everyone involved is on the same page.
- **Watch the alcohol and substances.** Know your own level and notice other people's.
- **Observers need guidelines too.** Everyone should feel comfortable and respected, whether they're participating or watching.
- **Organizers set the culture.** A clear code of conduct, education on consent, consequences for violations, and someone ready to step in when they see non-consensual behavior.

## Everyone's job

Consent is a shared responsibility, for every gender. Anyone can get it wrong. The most common mistakes:

- Assuming consent instead of hearing it
- Relying only on body language
- Pressuring or coercing
- Ignoring withdrawn consent
- Assuming one yes covers the whole encounter
- Misunderstanding what alcohol and substances do to someone's ability to consent
- Not checking in
- Ignoring nonverbal discomfort or hesitation
- Not saying your own boundaries out loud
- Not educating yourself

The fix for all ten: educate yourself, ask explicitly, check in, and respect the answer. Model it in your own relationships. Welcome questions about consent from your partners. If you see non-consensual behavior, intervene or get help.

## Teaching teens

If you're raising, teaching, or mentoring teenagers, start early and keep it going. Define consent clearly, in language they actually use. Walk through real-life scenarios and ask, "Is there consent here?"

Cover what adults sometimes skip:

- **Digital consent.** The same rules apply to texts, images, and anything shared online.
- **Peer pressure.** Teach them to recognize it and to make their own decisions.
- **Everyone.** Consent applies to every sexual orientation and gender identity.
- **Practice.** Role-plays and group discussions make it real.

Keep the door open so they come back with questions.

## When consent is missing

Sexual activity without consent can be a crime. How the law defines consent, incapacitation, and age varies by state and country. RAINN keeps a database where you can look up the laws in your state (RAINN, n.d.). None of this is legal advice.

The harm is real. Across nearly 200 studies, people who had been sexually assaulted reported worse mental health than people who hadn't, with the strongest links to posttraumatic stress and suicidal thoughts (Dworkin et al., 2017).

If it happened to you, it wasn't your fault. RAINN's National Sexual Assault Hotline is free, confidential, and open 24/7: **800-656-4673**, or chat at [rainn.org](https://rainn.org). In crisis in the U.S., call or text **988**.

## Try this

Before your next intimate moment, new partner or partner of twenty years, ask one question out loud that you'd normally assume the answer to. Then actually listen to the answer.

Ask. Listen. Honor the answer.
