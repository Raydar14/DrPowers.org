You picked up your phone to check one thing. Forty minutes later, you're still scrolling, and you can't remember what you came for.

You're not weak. These apps are built to keep you there. But when the urge to be online starts running your day, and your night, it costs you: sleep, mood, focus, and the life happening off the screen.

## What "digital addiction" means (and doesn't)

Researchers use "digital addiction" to describe compulsive, habitual, hard-to-control use of devices, and a pattern of doing the same online behavior over and over (Dresp-Langley & Hutt, 2022). It's a useful way to talk about a real problem.

It isn't a formal diagnosis, though. Here's where things stand:

- **Gaming disorder** is an official diagnosis in the World Health Organization's ICD-11. It means impaired control over gaming, gaming taking priority over other parts of life, and continuing despite harm, usually for at least 12 months. WHO notes it affects only a small proportion of people who play (WHO, 2020).
- **Internet gaming disorder** is in the DSM-5-TR only as a condition for further study. It covers gaming, not social media, smartphones, or general internet use (American Psychiatric Association, n.d.).

So if scrolling has taken over your life, you don't need a label for it to matter. You also don't need to panic that you "have an addiction."

## How it gets a grip

Researchers often describe addictive behavior with six core components (Griffiths, 2005):

1. **Salience.** It becomes the most important thing in your day.
2. **Mood modification.** You use it to change how you feel.
3. **Tolerance.** You need more of it to get the same effect.
4. **Withdrawal.** You feel irritable or restless when you can't do it.
5. **Conflict.** It causes problems with people, work, or your own goals.
6. **Relapse.** You cut back, then slide right back in.

If you recognize yourself in most of those, it's worth paying attention.

## What it does to sleep, mood, and memory

The connection with sleep is the strongest. A meta-analysis of 23 studies and more than 35,000 people found that people with internet addiction were about twice as likely to have sleep problems, and they slept less (Alimoradi et al., 2019).

Problematic use is also linked with emotional distress, depression, and memory problems (Dresp-Langley & Hutt, 2022). But "linked" is the key word. Most of this research is a snapshot in time, so it can't tell us which came first. Heavy use may wreck sleep and mood. Poor sleep and low mood may also drive heavy use. Probably both.

I've seen claims that compulsive screen use is the same as every other addiction, and that once it's out of control it always brings severe sleep loss and depression. The evidence doesn't support "always." Some researchers think digital addiction works like other addictions. Others disagree (Dresp-Langley & Hutt, 2022). What we can say: for a lot of people, it hurts.

## The dopamine question

You've probably heard of the "dopamine detox." The idea is that cutting out stimulation lets your brain's reward system reset.

Here's the honest version. Dopamine is a big part of how your brain learns what's rewarding and what to chase (Dresp-Langley & Hutt, 2022). Endless feeds and notifications tap into that system. That part is real.

The idea that a few days off "resets" your dopamine is mostly a metaphor. Neuroscientists say it's oversimplified: a break doesn't directly rewire your dopamine system, and it's not just dopamine anyway. Real change comes from building new habits, and that takes time (Thomasy, 2025).

I use the dopamine framing in my [sleep post](https://drpowers.org/blog/sleep-like-a-monk/) because it's a helpful picture of what's happening: less stimulation, steadier rhythm, fewer cravings. Just know it's a picture, not a lab result.

## Does each day get easier?

Here's the question I get most: "If I cut back on screen time, does each day get easier, like my brain is healing?"

For many people, yes, though not in a straight line.

The first few days are often the hardest. You reach for your phone without thinking. You feel bored, restless, a little cranky. That's normal. Your habits are still running the show.

Then something shifts. In one randomized trial, 467 adults blocked mobile internet on their phones for two weeks (calls and texts still worked). About 7 in 10 improved on mental health and well-being, and about 6 in 10 on sustained attention. For the group that blocked first, mental health and well-being were still better than at the start two weeks after the block ended (Castelo et al., 2025). In another trial, college students who limited social media to 10 minutes per app per day for three weeks were less depressed and lonely, especially those who were most depressed to begin with (Hunt et al., 2018).

Two important caveats. In the phone study, only about a quarter of people stuck to the block for most of the two weeks, and they saw the biggest gains (Castelo et al., 2025). And people in both studies knew what was being tested.

So: it does tend to get easier, and two to three weeks is a reasonable time to give it. That's not because your dopamine has "reset" on a schedule. It's because you're building new habits, sleeping more, and filling the space with real life.

## Try this

Pick one window each day, like the first hour after you wake up or the 90 minutes before bed, and make it phone-free for three weeks. Put the phone in another room. When the urge hits, notice it, name it ("there's the itch"), and let it pass.

Don't aim for perfect. Aim for most days.

Less scroll. More sleep. More you.

## If it's more than a habit

If you can't cut back no matter how hard you try, or your screen use is hurting your work, relationships, or mental health, talk to a licensed mental health professional. You don't need a diagnosis to deserve help.
