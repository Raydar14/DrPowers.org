Let's cut to the chase: non-consensual condom removal is a violation of consent, autonomy, and trust. I call it what it is. Condom rape.

It's not a "misunderstanding." It's not an "overreaction." It's a direct assault on a person's body and choices.

## Picture it

You agree to sex on one condition: a condom. Your partner agrees. Then, without telling you, they take it off.

That isn't "grey area" consent. You said yes to one thing, and something else happened to your body. It exposes you to unwanted pregnancy, sexually transmitted infections (including HIV), and real trauma.

That's not just disrespect. It's rape, plain and simple.

## How common is it?

More common than most people think. There's no single national number yet, so here's what the studies actually found, and who they asked.

- At a sexual health clinic in Melbourne, Australia, 32% of women and 19% of men who have sex with men said a partner had removed a condom without their consent (Latimer et al., 2018). That's 2,252 patients at one clinic, so it isn't the general population, but it's a lot of people.
- In a U.S. study of 503 women aged 21 to 30 who were recruited for research on sexual risk, about 12% said a male partner had done it to them (Davis et al., 2019).
- In a Canadian study of university students, nearly 1 in 5 of those who'd had sex with a man using a condom reported it (Czechowski et al., 2019).

Different samples, different numbers. Same message: this is not rare.

## The name is the point

Researchers and courts mostly say "non-consensual condom removal" or "stealthing." I say condom rape. Here's why.

**It puts consent at the center.** Everyone understands that rape means sex without consent. Consent to sex with a condom is not consent to sex without one. "Non-consensual condom removal" is technical. It leaves room for people to shrug. "Rape" doesn't.

**It tells the system how serious this is.** When lawmakers, police, and courts hear "rape," they know they're dealing with a sexual crime, not a bedroom disagreement. A clinical-sounding label makes it easier to treat it as something smaller.

**It carries the weight it deserves.** Neutral language has its place, in a journal article. "Rape" is intentionally not neutral. The harm isn't neutral either. And "stealthing" sounds sneaky, almost clever. There's nothing clever about it.

**It names the health risk.** Pregnancy, STIs, HIV. The word "rape" carries both the physical and the emotional danger, and makes clear this is a breach of safety, not just trust.

Here's the finding that convinces me most. In that Melbourne study, people who had been through it were less likely to call it sexual assault than people who hadn't. Among women, 62% of those it happened to called it assault, compared with 85% of those it hadn't happened to (Latimer et al., 2018). When the word is missing, survivors talk themselves out of their own experience.

## Awareness and accountability

The solution? Awareness and accountability. Women are standing up and calling this what it is: condom rape. We need to take it seriously, making sure men understand this isn't acceptable, and making sure women know they have every right to protect themselves.

## What the law says depends on where you live

The law hasn't caught up everywhere, and it varies a lot. A few examples:

- **United States.** California became the first state to address it directly in 2021 (AB 453), making it civil sexual battery, so survivors can sue. Maine and Washington have since passed similar civil laws (Washington State Legislature, 2024). A federal bill to let survivors sue was introduced in Congress in 2025 (Torres, 2025). Whether it can be charged as a crime depends on your state's sexual assault laws.
- **Canada.** In 2022, the Supreme Court ruled that when a partner makes a condom a condition of sex, sex without one is sex without consent under the Criminal Code (*R. v. Kirkpatrick*).
- **England and Wales.** Courts have treated breaking a condom condition as sex without consent (*Assange v Swedish Prosecution Authority*, 2011), and men have been convicted of rape for it, including a Bournemouth man jailed for 12 years in 2019 on charges that included rape (Grazia, 2019).
- **Elsewhere.** A Swiss court convicted a man of rape for it in 2017, and Switzerland's revised sexual offense law, in force since July 2024, covers it (swissinfo.ch, 2024). A German court convicted a man of sexual assault for it in 2018 (WTVR, 2018). A New Zealand court convicted a man of rape for it in 2021 (*R. v. Campos*, 2021). In Australia, the ACT made it a crime in 2021 (Twyford, 2021), and New South Wales, Victoria, Tasmania, South Australia, and Queensland have followed (Astor Legal, 2024; PinkNews, 2024).

None of this is legal advice. If you want to know your options, talk to a local advocate or attorney where it happened.

## If it happened to you

It was not your fault. It was not "normal." And you get to call it what it was.

- **Your health first.** See a doctor or a sexual health clinic as soon as you can. HIV prevention medicine (PEP) has to be started within 72 hours to work (CDC, n.d.), and you can ask about emergency contraception and STI testing at the same visit.
- **Talk to someone.** RAINN's National Sexual Assault Hotline is free, confidential, and open 24/7: **800-656-4673**, or chat at [rainn.org](https://rainn.org). In crisis in the U.S., call or text **988**.
- **Report it, talk about it, and don't let anyone dismiss it as "normal."** When and how you report is your decision.

## Try this

Say it out loud, to a friend, a partner, or your group chat: "Taking off a condom without consent is rape." Notice who flinches and who nods.

Awareness. Accountability. Consent, every time.
