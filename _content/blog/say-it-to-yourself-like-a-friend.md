You stand in front of the mirror and say, "I am enough."

And a voice in the back of your head says, "No, you're not."

If that's happened to you, you're not doing it wrong. The research on affirmations is mixed, and it explains exactly why that voice shows up.

## Why "I am" can backfire

In one well-known study, people with low self-esteem repeated "I'm a lovable person." Afterward, they felt worse, not better. People with high self-esteem felt better, but only slightly (Wood et al., 2009).

The likely reason: when a statement feels unbelievable, your brain argues back. Every "I am" gets an "Oh, really?" And the argument wins.

So repeating affirmations that feel untrue can backfire, especially for the people who want them most.

## Why "you are" may land softer

Here's the twist. How you talk to yourself matters, down to the pronoun.

When people talked to themselves using "you" or their own name instead of "I," they handled stressful tasks better. They gave better speeches, felt less anxious, and saw upcoming stress as more of a challenge and less of a threat (Kross et al., 2014). In another set of studies, people who gave themselves advice as "you" did better on a task and reported stronger intentions to exercise than people who used "I" (Dolcos & Albarracín, 2014).

Stepping a little outside your own perspective seems to help you regulate what you feel. Hearing "you" can feel more like support from someone in your corner, and less like a claim you have to defend.

That's an inference from the self-talk research, not a proven rule about affirmations. These studies were small and mostly done with college students. But it fits what I see: we are far kinder to our friends than to ourselves. So speak to yourself the way a friend would.

## How to use these

- **Pick three.** Not all of them. The three that feel true enough, or almost true.
- **Say them out loud.** Use "you," or your own name.
- **Let the doubt sit next to it.** In the same research, people did better when they were allowed to notice the negative thoughts too, instead of forcing only positive ones (Wood et al., 2009). You don't have to believe it fully yet.
