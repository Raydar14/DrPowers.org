Sex is healthy. Porn overuse is not.

Not all porn use is a problem. But fast, endless, high-volume online porn isn't a neutral activity for everyone. When it turns compulsive, it can change how you respond to arousal, how your body works in bed, and how you see yourself and the people you're with.

A note on who this is for: most of the research on porn and sexual problems has studied heterosexual men, so much of this post speaks to them. Anyone struggling with compulsive porn use, of any gender or orientation, is welcome here.

## It's not a character flaw. It's your reward system.

Each new scene or clip is a fresh hit of novelty. Your brain's reward system loves novelty. Over time, the link between arousal and a fast-changing screen gets stronger.

Then the brain adapts. The same content stops doing as much. You need more: more novelty, more time, more intensity, to get to the same place.

That's the theory behind "desensitization," and it's what I see in my work. Researchers are still testing how well it holds up. What's been measured so far: among men who watch porn, needing more and more viewing time to feel satisfied is closely tied to the core signs of problem use, like trouble resisting urges and using porn to cope with stress (Ince et al., 2024). Binge sessions and constant tab-jumping travel with it.

## The chase is the high, not the climax

Escalation is a pattern I see often in people who come to me for help. It can look like:

- Longer sessions, often edging for hours
- Aggressive or violent scenarios
- Content that conflicts with your own values or identity
- Taboo or disturbing material you never would have searched for at the start

For some, it moves off the screen: sexting, hiring sex workers, risky sex with strangers, or behavior that could be dangerous or illegal.

This isn't just about arousal. It's about compulsion. At this point, many people aren't chasing pleasure anymore. They're chasing relief, stimulation, or the feeling of being alive.

## What happens to real sex

Here's a serious consequence: real sex starts to feel underwhelming.

The slow pace, the emotional connection, and the unpredictability of a real partner don't match what the brain has been trained to expect. Real connection starts to feel "boring." Bodies, desire levels, and emotional needs vary in real life, and that can start to feel disappointing or frustrating. Consent, communication, and presence, which real intimacy depends on, can feel foreign.

Research backs up part of this. Across 50 studies and about 50,000 people, men who watched more porn reported lower sexual and relationship satisfaction. Women, on average, didn't (Wright et al., 2017). Those studies show a link, not a cause. Unhappy men may also turn to porn more.

In porn, women are often shown as always available, eager to please, and on the receiving end of aggression. In one analysis of 304 scenes from best-selling videos, 88% included physical aggression, mostly by men toward women (Bridges et al., 2010). When that's the main sexual script in your life, it can shape what you expect from real partners.

That isn't just unfair to the people you're with. It's isolating for you. It keeps sex stuck in a performance model instead of an experience of vulnerability and give-and-take.

Partners often tell me they feel objectified, unwanted, or emotionally shut out, even when no physical cheating has happened. And the shame of that disconnect often feeds more withdrawal and secrecy, which deepens the cycle.

## Porn and erections: what we know and what we don't

You've probably heard of "porn-induced erectile dysfunction." Here's the honest version: it's a proposed idea, not a recognized diagnosis, and researchers disagree about it.

**The case for a link.** Erection problems aren't just an older man's issue. At one clinic in Italy, about one in four men newly diagnosed with ED were 40 or younger, and nearly half of those had severe ED (Capogrosso et al., 2013). In an international online survey of sexually active men aged 18 to 35, about 21% reported some degree of ED, and men with higher scores for problematic porn use were more likely to have it (Jacobs et al., 2021). Notice what predicted ED there: problematic use, not simply how often someone watched.

**The case against.** Large surveys of younger men in Europe found little evidence that how often men watch porn is tied to erection problems (Landripet & Štulhofer, 2015). A survey of more than 3,500 men found that age, anxiety, depression, health problems, and relationship satisfaction predicted ED. Porn use didn't (Rowland et al., 2022). A review of the observational research found no solid evidence that porn causes ED, and pointed out that men with ED may simply watch more porn (Dwulit & Rzymski, 2019).

**Where that leaves you.** The evidence is mixed, and most of it can't prove cause and effect. In my clinical view, the men who struggle most are the ones whose use has become compulsive: they can respond to porn but not to a partner, and sex has become a source of anxiety. Performance anxiety leads to avoiding intimacy, which leads to more solo porn. Over time, sex becomes something you do alone, and closeness starts to feel like a threat instead of a desire.

If you're having erection problems, see a medical provider too. ED can show up years before heart disease, and it shares risk factors like diabetes, so it deserves a real workup, not just a guess (Mayo Clinic, 2011).

## When it becomes a disorder

When porn use becomes compulsive, it can meet the criteria for compulsive sexual behaviour disorder (CSBD). The World Health Organization added CSBD to its diagnostic manual, the ICD-11, as code 6C72, in the impulse control disorders (World Health Organization, 2019; Kraus et al., 2018).

CSBD means a persistent pattern of failing to control intense, repeated sexual urges, lasting six months or more and causing marked distress or real problems in your life. It can look like:

- Sex or porn becoming the center of your life, to the point of neglecting your health, self-care, or responsibilities
- Trying again and again to cut back, without success
- Continuing despite serious consequences
- Continuing even when you're getting little satisfaction from it

Many people also use porn to numb or escape painful feelings. That's not part of the official definition, but it's one of the strongest patterns tied to problem use (Ince et al., 2024).

One part of the definition I love: distress that comes only from moral judgment or disapproval of your own sexuality doesn't count (World Health Organization, 2019). Research supports that line. A lot of people feel "addicted" mainly because their porn use clashes with their values, not because they've lost control (Grubbs et al., 2019). That's a real struggle, and it deserves care. It just isn't the same thing as compulsion.

## How common is it?

Hard to pin down. Older estimates suggested 3 to 6% of adults struggle with compulsive sexual behavior, and more recent studies suggest 1 to 3% (Kraus et al., 2018). In a national U.S. survey of 2,325 adults, 8.6% screened positive for clinically significant distress about controlling their sexual urges and behavior: 10.3% of men and 7.0% of women (Dickenson et al., 2018). That was a screening tool, not a diagnosis, but it tells you this isn't rare.

For many people, porn isn't the only outlet. It's part of a wider pattern of compulsive sexual thinking, fantasizing, and behavior.

## What it can cost you

In my work, I see unchecked porn overuse lead to:

- Loss of desire for real-life partners
- Trouble feeling pleasure in everyday life
- Emotional numbness, shame, and self-loathing
- Relationship breakdown, infidelity, and divorce
- Lost confidence, motivation, and personal integrity

Most dangerously, you become isolated, living in a fantasy world that requires no vulnerability, no risk, and no growth. The longer it goes on, the more disconnected you get from yourself and the people who love you.

## A word about the industry

The industry has real ethical problems, and they matter. In 2025, the owner of GirlsDoPorn was sentenced to 27 years in federal prison for sex trafficking after his company used misleading modeling ads and false promises to coerce hundreds of young women into filming (U.S. Department of Justice, 2025). Easy access for minors is another concern, because porn can become a young person's first lesson about sex.

## Try this today

Next time you feel the pull, pause before you open anything. Name what you're feeling: bored, lonely, stressed, tired, wired. Write it down.

You don't have to stop yet. Just notice. Relapse isn't failure. It's data. So is every urge.
